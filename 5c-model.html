01
C1 — Coaching

What should a founder settle before going abroad?

Coaching is the first C because six things should be settled before any foreign company is registered: where to go first, who buys, what has to change in the product, how the venture is funded, who owns it, and what result would make the business stop. None of these needs a company to exist. All of them change what kind of company you should form, if any.

Explore C1 Coaching →
  1. Where first? Not where demand is largest, but where your product has a reason to win and the cost of entry fits your balance sheet.
  2. Who buys, and who decides? The end user, the importer, the procurement head and the regulator are often four different people.
  3. What has to change? Specifications, certifications, labelling, packaging, pricing, payment terms and the website all get tested against local expectations.
  4. How will it be funded? Out of the Indian company's cash flow, through a foreign raise, or through a partner.
  5. Who owns it? A foreign venture run in the founder's spare hours has the wrong owner.
  6. What does “not working” look like? A pre-agreed point at which you stop, pause or change course.

Effects are larger on self-reported outcomes than on observed ones, a fair warning against coaching that only makes people feel better. The realistic baseline for seminars is modest, which is why we coach one founder and one market at a time.

Most of the expensive surprises in a new market are local, not legal. A product that sells on price in India may need to sell on certification in Germany. Packaging that works on an Indian shelf may break labelling rules in the UK. A website written for Indian buyers may read as untrustworthy to an American procurement team that expects named people, a local address and prices in dollars.

  2. Copying a competitor's route. Their entry made sense for their product, margins and timeline. Yours may not.
  3. Treating the website as an afterthought. For many foreign buyers the website is the first due-diligence check.
  4. Leaving the family out of the plan. In family businesses, succession and ownership questions travel with the expansion.

C2 — Consulting

How do you structure a foreign business so it stays compliant in both countries?

Consulting turns the market-entry map into a legal and financial structure that two governments will accept: the entity type, who owns it, where profits are taxed, how money moves, and which filings fall due every year in the host country and in India. It is the largest of the five Cs.

Explore C2 Consulting →

An Indian company that sets up abroad answers to two regulators from the first rupee it sends. The foreign side wants a registered entity, a tax number, a bank account and annual filings. The Indian side, under FEMA and the RBI's overseas investment rules, wants to know about the investment before and after it happens.

Most cross-border mistakes happen in the space between those two rulebooks. A foreign accountant files the American return and has never heard of an Annual Performance Report. An Indian CA files the Form ODI and assumes the foreign side is someone else's job. Each is correct within their slice. The business still ends up non-compliant.

Rules also move. A final FinCEN rule effective 14 August 2026 permanently exempts US-formed companies, including US subsidiaries of Indian firms, from beneficial ownership reporting. A compliance calendar built in 2024 is already out of date.

The fee is modest: a ₹5 crore ODI remittance reported two years late costs about ₹32,500. The real cost is the bar that comes with it. Until the delay is fixed, no further financial commitment abroad is allowed, so a subsidiary that needs more capital from its Indian parent simply cannot receive it.

C3 — Connecting

How do you find the right partners in a market where nobody knows you?

Connecting gives a business what it cannot buy with a registration fee: qualified introductions to the distributors, agents, suppliers, investors, bankers and officials who decide whether a newcomer gets a meeting at all.

Explore C3 Connecting →

Being foreign costs something. Being outside the network costs more. In the revised Uppsala model of internationalisation, Johanson and Vahlne replaced the old “liability of foreignness” with a “liability of outsidership”: a firm inside the right business network learns faster and is trusted sooner, wherever it comes from.

The diaspora does not trade on India's behalf by default. A 2025 IIFT study found Indian emigrants help for certain goods and in poorer host countries, where their market knowledge fills real gaps. The diaspora has to be organised and matched.

C4 — Collaboration

How do you make a cross-border partnership work after the contract is signed?

Collaboration is where an introduction becomes a working operation: goods cleared and stored, staff hired and paid, and a written agreement that says who does what and how either side can leave.

Explore C4 Collaboration →

The failures in this phase are rarely dramatic. They are small and repeated, cheap to prevent and expensive to unwind:

  1. A product classified under the wrong HS code pays the wrong duty on every shipment until an audit finds it.
  2. An importer of record unfamiliar with a marketplace's inbound rules leaves stock outside a fulfilment centre while storage fees run.
  3. A contractor abroad is paid for months without an agreement that assigns the intellectual property they create.
  4. A distributor is appointed for the whole country with no sales targets, and the exclusivity outlives the enthusiasm.
Harvard's David Arnold followed eight companies through almost 250 market entries. Distributor sales start well, then plateau; the exporter buys back the rights at great cost. His advice: plan the relationship from the start, with the exporter directing marketing strategy from day one.

C5 — Co-creation

When should an adviser become a co-owner of your foreign venture?

Co-creation is the stage at which Connect Ventures stops advising from outside the venture and shares in it, through co-investment, a joint venture, an acquisition or a long-term operating partnership. It is reserved for businesses that have already proved their product and tested their market through the first four Cs.

Explore C5 Co-creation →

Co-creation is the right answer to a narrow set of problems. It fits when four things are true:

  1. The product already sells profitably at home, and early sales abroad confirm demand.
  2. The gap is capital, ownership or long-term operating capacity, not knowledge.
  3. Both sides bring something the other cannot easily buy: market access, technology, licences, customers or management time.
  4. The founders are willing to share decisions, information and, eventually, the exit.
It does not fit a business still searching for its first foreign customer, a founder who wants money without a partner, or a venture whose main asset is a promise. Those businesses need Coaching or Connecting first, and a good adviser says so before any term sheet is drafted.

Most of the damage happens in the launch phase, from the memorandum of understanding through the first 100 days. A launch has to align the parents' strategies, create shared governance, manage the economic ties between parents and venture, and build one organisation out of two.

You will often see a claim that 50 to 70 per cent of joint ventures fail. We could not trace that figure to a primary study, so we do not use it.

For an Indian investor, every form of co-creation abroad is an overseas investment under FEMA. The 2022 rules:

400%Cap on an Indian entity's total financial commitment abroad, as a share of its net worth.RBI Master Direction, 2022
2 layersLimit on subsidiaries in structures that invest back into India, with reporting at each step.RBI Master Direction, 2022

If the Indian investor has a reporting delay that is not regularised, the venture cannot receive further money from India until it is fixed. Compliance is a commercial question for every partner, not only a legal one.
